Case study / IQVIA

Territory Designer

Every pharmaceutical sales rep gets a patch of the map to cover. Splitting a country into those patches fairly is harder than it sounds, because an even split by workload is almost never an even split by earning potential, and neither one gives you sensible drive times. I designed the tool that lets a strategist try different versions, see what each one costs, and show their work before anyone signs off.

01

The problem

Territory design is the work of balancing workload against sales potential to build territories that are compact enough to cover, while disturbing the existing sales force as little as possible. Those three goals pull against each other constantly. Give a rep a tidier drive and you have probably handed their best accounts to somebody else, and a territory is somebody's income, so getting it wrong costs you people as well as coverage.

The brief was to build that tool for global use. It had to run on whatever geography a market actually used, taking shape files from global and local vendors, so the same interface had to handle American postal codes, European bricks and everything in between without becoming a different product in each country.

Two use cases drove it. A new product or a new indication means standing up a sales force from nothing and sizing it, or realigning an existing one to cover new specialties. Separately, a structure already in the field needs periodic rebalancing as physicians move, reps move and the business changes, all inside a defined limit on how much any one rep's patch is allowed to shift.

02

The decisions

Decision 01

Read the wireframes for intent, then build the spine

I was not starting from a blank page, and I did not want to. The wireframes already knew things about this business that I would have taken months to learn. The job was to work out what they were reaching for and then build it properly.

What came out of that reading was the navigation: Create, Refine, Review, Import. It is not a menu of features, it is the actual life of a territory plan, and once it was the permanent spine of the app every screen had an obvious home. The dashboard then had one job, which was to get you back to the scenario you were in the middle of.

Decision 02

Treat the map as the document, not as a panel

Refining is where the real work happens, so the map gets the whole canvas and everything else sits on top of it in panels that collapse. The territory outlines, the provider and account pins, the sector labels: that is the document the strategist is editing, and it should never be squeezed into a viewport beside a form.

The numbers that move when you edit, coverage and headcount, sit at the top of the left panel where your eye already is after every change. The tools are a short list on purpose. Lasso, undo, redo, delete, plus layer toggles for providers and accounts. Everything else waits behind Optimize, Parameters and Change Log rather than crowding the thing you are pointing at.

Decision 03

Show the outliers, not the average

A plan that averages beautifully can still contain the two territories that cause every argument. So the analysis view plots each territory as a point, sales against driving distance, draws the acceptable band across it, and rings everything that falls outside. Tabs carry the other cuts of the same question: customers to sales area, geography to sales area, the employee roster.

Averages hide the conversation you are about to have with a regional manager. The outliers are that conversation, so they are what the screen leads with, and each one opens into the detail you would need to defend or fix it.

Decision 04

Make importing somebody else's data a designed step

Every client arrives with their own columns, named their own way, and none of it matches the schema. In most enterprise tools that becomes a support ticket. Here it is a screen: the incoming file on the left, the schema on the right, drag to match, and save the mapping so the next upload from the same client takes one click.

It is unglamorous and it is the difference between a tool a consultant can hand to a client and one they have to run on the client's behalf forever.

03

Designed inside Apollo

IQVIA had a design system, Apollo, and Territory Designer is built from it: type scale, color, cards, form rows, inputs, charts, the lot. Working inside a system on a product this specific is mostly a question of what you do when the system does not have your component, and a map canvas with a lasso tool is not in anybody's UI kit.

So the rule I worked to was that anything Apollo covered came from Apollo unchanged, and anything it did not was built to look like it would have if Apollo had got there first. That kept the product recognisably part of the platform, and it meant the map tools did not read as a bolted-on third-party control, which is exactly what they would have become if I had designed them on their own terms.

04

Where it got to

The work ran from those first wireframes through to a complete interactive prototype. The exported build comes to 496 screens across login, dashboard, model and scenario creation, refinement and optimization, browsing, review and feedback, import and export, which is every state an engineer would need to build against rather than a walkthrough of the happy path.

The part I would carry into any tool like this: a territory plan is an argument someone has to win in a room. The interface is only doing its job if it hands them the evidence in the shape the argument actually takes.
