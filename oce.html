Case study / IQVIA

Orchestrated Customer Engagement

A pharmaceutical sales rep gets a couple of minutes in a physician's doorway, and everything they log afterward is a regulated record. I led the UI design for the iPad CRM they use to do it, from requirements and flows through to the shipped product.

Role
UI design lead, team of five
Client
IQVIA
Platform
iPad, field-first
Users
Sales reps, MSLs, account managers, admins
Partners
Product management, engineering, visual design
Shipped
December 2017, into 130+ countries

The shipped product. Customer dashboard, module launcher, and a nudge to finish a call the rep started on Friday.

01

The problem

Field software in life sciences usually gets built for the audit rather than for the rep. Samples handed to a physician count against limits. Meetings need approvals from several levels. Every rejection and cancellation has to carry a reason, a name and a timestamp. The previous generation of tools pushed all of that onto the rep, so calls got logged from memory hours later, or not at all.

Product management framed the goal in one line at the top of the flow document: more efficient calls. That was the measure, and it shaped how I read every requirement that followed. Anything that added a tap without adding certainty was working against it.

02

What I was handed

Product management gave me requirements and use cases, not screens. For the meetings work that meant five personas and seven user stories, written as plain statements of what each role must be able to do.

Reading them properly took a while, because the roles overlap. A sales rep can organize a meeting. So can a medical science liaison, an account manager, or a consumer health sales rep. An account manager is usually the one approving it. The same person can be organizer on one meeting and approver on the next, which means the interface cannot key off job title. It has to key off what you are to this meeting.

03

The decisions

Decision 01

Turn the list of stories into a map

Seven stories in a list look like seven small jobs. Drawn as a map they stop looking independent, because the rules between them show up. A meeting can be rejected more than once, by different approvers, sometimes for the same reason. A cancellation can only happen once. Rejection reasons travel from the approver's account to the organizer's, and cancellations go out to every attendee.

None of that is in the stories. All of it changes the design. So the first thing I produced was the epic map, with the system's own actions lettered alongside the user's, and the rules written down beside it where product, engineering and I could argue about them in one place.

Decision 02

Give every story its own screen-by-screen flow

Each story then got drawn as a sequence, with every screen numbered and every action lettered. Rejecting a meeting is six steps: meeting preview, details tab, actions drop-down, review meeting, reasons drop-down, confirmation. Written that way it is countable, and engineering can estimate it without a meeting.

The numbering does a second job. A QA analyst can walk 2.1 through 2.6 as a test script, and a support ticket can say "breaks at 3.5" instead of describing a screen. Those numbers stayed in use long after the design work was done.

Decision 03

Wireframe in grey, and design the error at the same time as the happy path

Every screen in the flow got a wireframe with the taps called out in order. Working in grey keeps the conversation on what the screen is for rather than what the button should look like, and it makes changes cheap while they are still cheap.

It also let me design the failure alongside the success. The story said the system must enforce at least one rejection reason. That is one sentence in a requirements doc and two screens in reality: the error state that tells the approver what is missing, and the confirmation that tells them it went to the organizer. Both got drawn in the same pass, so neither arrived late as a patch.

Decision 04

Design the record, not just the transaction

A rejection is not one event. A meeting can bounce between district managers, regional managers, home office, finance and legal, and the organizer has to understand the whole chain to fix it. So the history is a sortable table of every transaction: what happened, who did it, when, the comment they left, and the status it moved from and to.

I also gave people two ways to reach it, because they arrive from two directions. If you are looking at the meeting, it is a tab. If you saw the notification first, the notification takes you straight there. Cancelled meetings come off the planner, but they show up in the drawer, so nothing disappears without saying why.

04

Leading the work

I led a team of five UX/UI designers, split by surface. Most of that job is not critiquing screens, it is keeping five people's instincts pointed in the same direction, which is why the documents above are all built the same way. Anyone on the team could pick up an epic and know what a finished spec looked like.

I worked directly with engineering and the product managers through implementation as well, because on a product like this a requirement that ships slightly wrong is a compliance bug, not a cosmetic one. And I sat with the visual design team so the product read as the same brand as the marketing around it.

Consistency at this scale is a coordination problem before it's a design problem.

05

What engineering got

The specs were only half the handover. The other half was the UI guidelines: named color tokens, a type scale from 40px headline down to 11px caption with every step labelled, an icon font, and a worked example of the layout.

Naming things is the point. Handing engineering $color-theme-neptune and headline-2, Semibold 32px means a change to the ramp moves everywhere at once, and nobody has to eyedrop a comp to find out what blue this is.

06

The result

The same structure runs through every surface. A left rail that never moves, a notification drawer that is always one tap away, and a working area that changes without the rep losing their place.

07

How it landed

OCE launched on 12 December 2017. Within twelve months more than thirty life sciences companies had picked it up, Novo Nordisk and Roche among them, and it was running in 115 countries. By 2024 it served close to 400 enterprise customers across more than 130.

130+Countries

That reach is the argument for working this way. Approval chains, reason lists and enforcement rules are not the same in Japan, Germany and the United States. Because each one was specified as a rule attached to a shared pattern rather than drawn as a fixed screen, a market with a different chain gets a different list and the same product.

In April 2024 IQVIA licensed the OCE CRM software to Salesforce, which folded it into Salesforce Life Sciences Cloud, and committed to supporting existing OCE customers through 2029. So the work outlived the product it was built for, which doesn't happen often.

One thing I took with me. The hard part was never fitting the rules onto a screen. It was working out which ones a person should have to remember, and the answer was usually none of them.
